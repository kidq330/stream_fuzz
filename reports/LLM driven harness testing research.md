# Let Engines Judge: Harness Loops Beat Raw Inspection

Yes: for finding bugs that a maintainer will accept, an LLM is better used as a driver that writes harnesses, properties, mutants, models and specs for a classical engine than as a token-hungry reader of code, and the 2023–2026 literature supports this not with a single controlled experiment (none exists) but with a uniform pattern across every ablation, benchmark and production system examined. Function-level reading benchmarks in 2024 put LLM vulnerability classification at **balanced accuracy 0.50–0.55** and **pairwise accuracy 8–11%**, and every credible real-world result since then (Big Sleep, Aardvark, Anthropic/Mozilla, AIxCC, OSS-Fuzz-Gen, Antithesis) inserts an executable verdict (sanitizer crash, proof-of-vulnerability, property failure, model-checker counterexample) before anything is reported; where raw reports were filed without one, curl's bounty validity rate fell **below 5%** and the program closed. Within hybrid systems the ablations attribute precision to the engine and specification-authoring to the model: IRIS doubles CodeQL's detections only when the LLM mines the taint specs, Agentless beats costlier agents by execution-filtered patch selection at **$0.34 per issue**, and Team Atlanta's AIxCC fuzzer produced **48 unique PoVs versus 25 from LLM modules**. Quality of these loops is now measured by mutation score, fail-to-pass on real bugs, property soundness and vacuity checks rather than coverage, because LLM tests reach **35% coverage but only 19% mutation score** and a suite can hit **100% coverage at 4% mutation score**; the harness route has its own false-positive channel (**57–65%** of early OSS-Fuzz-Gen crashes were harness-induced) and the spec route its own vacuity channel (**34.6%** of verifier-passing specs are incomplete), so "engine says fail" is only a verdict if the harness, property or spec has itself been validated, typically by mutation. Commercially, TesterArmy and the rest of the venture-funded QA-agent cohort are pure inspectors with no engine underneath; Antithesis is the only vendor shipping an explicit LLM-writes-workload, engine-searches, mutant-validates loop, and no company sells LLM-authored property-based tests gated by a mutation score, which, together with the BEAM ecosystem and in-process concurrency exploration, is the clearest unexploited gap. Of the user's nine unshipped merges from September 2026, only the XState model-based-testing piece has partly shipped (XState v6, 28 Sep 2026); mutation-graded properties, example-to-property promotion and the bugs-per-CPU-hour benchmark remain open and are the most promising next moves for stream_fuzz, whose own README caveat ("I wouldn't confidently say a regular property-testing suite wouldn't [find them]") is precisely the measurement the field has not made.

## Every credible bug-hunting result since 2024 ends in an executed verdict

The baseline against which the driver approach must be judged is what a model achieves by reading alone, and through 2024 that baseline was close to chance on hard data. Steenhoek et al. evaluated eleven LLMs with zero-shot, few-shot, contrastive and chain-of-thought prompting and found **balanced accuracy between 0.50 and 0.55 for every model from 7B to GPT-4-turbo, with no scaling law** ([arXiv 2403.17218](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/a-comprehensive-study-of-the-capabilities-of-large-language-models-for/wrk_59c89fc1065b88e18df3c21879e45027), search-summary). SecLLMHolmes (IEEE S&P 2024) showed that merely renaming identifiers flipped verdicts in **26% of PaLM2 and 17% of GPT-4 cases** and concluded LLMs are "not yet ready to be used for automated vulnerability detection" ([arXiv 2312.12575](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.12575v3), peer-reviewed). PrimeVul's paired evaluation, which counts a case only if both the vulnerable function and its patch are classified correctly, puts LLM pairwise accuracy at **8.28–10.57%**, and a 7B model that scored 68% F1 on the noisy BigVul set fell to **3.09% F1** on PrimeVul ([arXiv 2403.18624](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18624), search-summary; the notes could not open the paper to confirm the F1 collapse). The 2026 benchmark literature has not reversed this: SastBench measures agentic triage at **precision 0.14–0.17 for recall 0.58–0.72**, with weaker models scoring below random ([arXiv 2601.02941](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.02941), search-summary), and a 2026 dual-mode benchmark reports **10–50% false-positive rates for every frontier model** in white-box detection ([arXiv 2605.23243](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23243v1), search-summary).

Against this, 2025–2026 produced spectacular real-world finds, and it is important to read what they do and do not show. Google's Big Sleep agent carried a code browser, a sandboxed Python interpreter to generate inputs, and a debugger; its SQLite stack underflow and the July 2025 CVE-2025-6965 were found by an agent that executed, not one that read ([Project Zero](https://projectzero.google/2024/10/from-naptime-to-big-sleep.html), search-summary). OpenAI's Aardvark validates each candidate "in a sandbox by attempting to trigger the vuln" before reporting and published no precision figure ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-aardvark/), vendor). Anthropic's Firefox engagement is the most transparent vendor datapoint: Claude Opus 4.6 scanned nearly 6,000 C++ files, submitted **112 unique reports**, of which **22 became confirmed vulnerabilities** (14 high), used "task verifiers" and isolated-VM validation before filing, and spent roughly **$4,000 for two working exploits out of several hundred attempts** ([Anthropic/Mozilla](https://www.anthropic.com/news/mozilla-firefox-security), fetched, vendor). That is a roughly 20% report-to-accepted-vulnerability rate even after the vendor's own executable validation, with the remainder being crashes of unclear security impact. The headline Opus 4.6 claim of "more than 500 previously unknown high-severity" bugs "without task-specific tooling, custom scaffolding, or specialized prompting" ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/02/claude-opus-46-finds-500-high-severity.html), vendor, search-summary) and the Mythos Preview figures, **23,019 flagged, 6,202 estimated high/critical, >90% of 1,752 assessed findings validated**, ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/05/26/anthropic-project-glasswing-update/), vendor, search-summary) show that frontier models can *propose* well from reading; but both pipelines still pay for validation, the Mythos per-finding cost and triage burden were not published, and curl's maintainer called the announcement a "PR stunt" ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/curl-bug-bounty-ai-security-reports-daniel-stenberg/), search-summary). No independent, peer-reviewed precision measurement of Opus 4.6, Mythos, Aardvark or Big Sleep exists in the notes; the one third-party run found Opus 4.6 catching **~28% of 435 known CVEs at a false-positive rate above 40%** ([ZeroPath](https://zeropath.com/blog/ai-coding-assistants-are-not-sast), vendor, search-summary).

The receiving side has already voted. curl's HackerOne program confirmed 87 real vulnerabilities over seven years, but its validity rate "ran north of 15 percent, then in 2025 plummeted to below 5 percent" and the program closed in January 2026 ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/curl-ending-bug-bounty-program-after-flood-of-ai-slop-reports/), search-summary). The Linux kernel security list went from **2–3 reports a week in 2024 to 5–10 a day by March 2026**, with Torvalds calling it "almost entirely unmanageable" and kernel documentation now requiring AI-assisted reports to be "tested, and fact-based" ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/113716-ai-finds-1500-vulnerabilities-linux-kernel-linus-torvalds.html), search-summary). HackerOne's own data cuts the other way for validated submissions: of >1,100 "hackbot" reports in five months, "nearly half were valid" ([HackerOne](https://www.hackerone.com/blog/ai-hackbots-security-testing-update), vendor, search-summary), which is ten times curl's rate and consistent with hackbots that validate before filing. Maintainers' revealed preference is for the artifact a harness loop emits, a reproducer, and against the artifact a comment-only reviewer emits.

The ablations inside hybrid systems are where the division of labour becomes measurable. IRIS (ICLR 2025) has the LLM infer CWE-specific taint sources and sinks, CodeQL propagate them, and the LLM filter alerts; on CWE-Bench-Java it detects **55 vulnerabilities to CodeQL's 27**, and replacing either the LLM-inferred source or sink specs with CodeQL's built-ins yields "significantly fewer vulnerabilities" ([arXiv 2405.17238](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.17238v3), peer-reviewed, search-summary). LLift (OOPSLA 2024) takes ~1,000 static-analysis candidates that timed out in symbolic execution and reaches **50% precision with no missed bugs**, finding 13 new kernel UBI bugs ([OOPSLA PDF](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~zhiyunq/pub/oopsla24_llift.pdf), peer-reviewed, search-summary). RepoAudit (ICML 2025) attributes its **78–88% precision** to a validator that checks data-flow facts and path-condition satisfiability behind the exploring agent, at **$2.54 and 0.44 hours per project** ([arXiv 2501.18160](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18160v1), peer-reviewed, search-summary). On the test-writing side, SWT-Bench (NeurIPS 2024) shows that "directly prompting an LLM creates bug reproduction tests successfully in only **3.6%** of cases" ([swt-bench README](https://github.com/logic-star-ai/swt-bench), repo), while Agentless reaches 27.3% on SWE-bench Lite at **$0.34 per issue** by generating reproduction tests and re-ranking patches on execution outcomes, beating AutoCodeRover at $1.33 ([Agentless README](https://github.com/OpenAutoCoder/Agentless), repo). AssertFlip tops the SWT-Bench leaderboard by having the LLM write *passing* tests on buggy behaviour and then inverting the assertions, because "LLMs are better at writing passing tests than ones that crash or fail on purpose" ([arXiv 2507.17542](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17542), search-summary): a telling observation about what the model is good at (characterising behaviour) versus what the engine is for (deciding).

DARPA's AIxCC final (August 2025) is the closest thing to a field experiment. Its scoring applied an accuracy multiplier of 1 − (1 − r)^4, so a system at 40% accuracy lost 13% of its score and unverified reporting was economically punished ([AIxCC SoK site](https://github.com/occia/aixcc-sok-webpage), repo). Every finalist converged on "LLM proposes, execution judges": Trail of Bits' Buttercup is "an AI/ML-assisted fuzzing campaign (built on oss-fuzz)" with an LLM seed generator and patcher ([Buttercup](https://github.com/trailofbits/buttercup), repo); Texas A&M's FuzzingBrain states "Nothing is reported that has not crashed a real build" ([fuzzingbrain](https://github.com/fuzzingbrain/afc-crs-all-you-need-is-a-fuzzing-brain), repo). The winning Atlantis team published the only quantitative attribution: in its Multilang component the plain fuzzer produced **48 unique PoVs, the LLM-integrated input generators 25**, with the LLM paths credited for format-aware bugs the fuzzer struggled on, and the team noted "smaller models like GPT-4o-mini often outperformed larger foundation models" for their sub-tasks ([Team Atlanta](https://team-atlanta.github.io/blog/post-crs-multilang/), repo blog). The notes carry two incompatible aggregate figures for the final, **54 of 63** synthetic vulnerabilities per one source and **43 of 70** per another ([DARPA](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results), search-summary); the discrepancy is unresolved and both should be treated as approximate. Average cost was **$152 per task** with ~45 minutes to remediation ([DARPA](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results), search-summary).

The steelman for raw inspection deserves stating. SastBench found Gemini 2.5 Pro *without tools* matched or exceeded some ReAct agents on recall ([arXiv 2601.02941](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.02941), search-summary); Carlini's per-file "CTF" loop uses no fuzzer in discovery and is credited with 500+ zero-days, confirming each with an exploit afterwards ([SCW podcast](https://securitycryptographywhatever.com/2026/03/25/ai-bug-finding/), search-summary); and Snyk's commentary on Claude Code Security credits a CGIF heap overflow to "reasoning about the LZW compression algorithm, something traditional coverage-guided fuzzing couldn't catch even with 100% code coverage" ([Snyk](https://snyk.io/articles/anthropic-launches-claude-code-security/), search-summary). The honest synthesis is that discovery can now happen either by model reading or by engine search, and that frontier models have become strong proposers; but the open question is *where discovery is cheapest per confirmed bug*, and no paper in the notes fixes a dollar budget and runs the same frontier model as raw reviewer versus harness author on identical targets. OSS-Fuzz-Gen's README lists cost as "None", Mythos published no per-finding cost, and no "bugs per CPU-hour versus bugs per million tokens" study exists ([oss-fuzz-gen](https://github.com/google/oss-fuzz-gen), repo). That is the central missing experiment, and it maps directly onto the benchmark the user's September notes called for.

## Coverage is gameable, mutation score measures sensitivity, and neither measures truth

If the loop's quality is judged by the engine's own metric, the metric has to be one the model cannot satisfy vacuously, and the literature has spent three years discovering which metrics those are. TestGenEval (ICLR 2025) found the best model, GPT-4o, reaching **35.2% coverage but only 18.8% mutation score** on real repositories ([testgeneval](https://github.com/facebookresearch/testgeneval), repo; figures search-summary). MutGen reports a HumanEval-Java subject with **100% line and branch coverage and a 4% mutation score** ([arXiv 2506.02954](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02954v8), search-summary); an OOPSLA 2026 paper motivates itself with LLM suites at "≈80% coverage but only ≈35% mutation score" ([ACM 10.1145/3798251](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3798251), peer-reviewed, search-summary); and YATE found a *plain* LLM (33.8% mutation score) beating the coverage-optimised HITS and SymPrompt (≈28%), a concrete case of coverage bought at the expense of assertion strength ([arXiv 2507.18316](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18316v1), search-summary). Meta's production experience follows the same arc: TestGen-LLM (FSE 2024) filtered by coverage increase and only 25% of generated tests raised coverage ([arXiv 2402.09171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171), peer-reviewed, search-summary); ACH (FSE 2025) switched the oracle to killing an LLM-written, concern-specific mutant, applied it to 10,795 Kotlin classes producing 9,095 mutants and 571 hardening tests, with **73% engineer acceptance** and an equivalent-mutant detector at **precision 0.79 / recall 0.47, rising to 0.95 / 0.96 with simple pre-processing** ([arXiv 2501.12862](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12862), peer-reviewed, search-summary). The notes could not read what the pre-processing is, which is the single most important replication detail.

Mutation score, however, has its own blind spot, and two ISSTA 2026 papers from the same group make it precise. The replicability study concludes that coverage and mutation "can provide meaningful signals ... in regression-style settings where the code provided to the LLM can be reasonably assumed bug-free," but "in scenarios where the code-under-test may already be buggy and the goal is to expose the bug, they no longer serve as reliable indicators" ([arXiv 2607.22880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22880), peer-reviewed, search-summary). The companion paper names the mechanism, the "misguidance effect": prompting with buggy code produces tests that assert the bug, and the mitigation that works is to replace the code in the prompt with an LLM-generated specification docstring ([arXiv 2607.22883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22883), peer-reviewed, search-summary). A software-evolution study over 22,374 program variants quantifies the same thing: **more than 99% of tests that fail after a semantic change still pass on the original program**, meaning regenerated tests are characterisation tests of whatever the code did ([arXiv 2603.23443](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.23443v1), search-summary). The practical consequence for a driver design is that mutation gating guarantees non-vacuity, not truth: a test that asserts a buggy return value still kills the mutant that changes it. The oracle must come from somewhere other than the implementation (documentation, a requirement, a property, a model), which is exactly the artifact the LLM is asked to write in the driver paradigm.

Property-based testing has its own three-part rubric, established by PBT-GPT (Vikram, Lemieux, Sunshine, Padhye): *validity* (runs on generated inputs), *soundness* (does not fail on correct code) and *property coverage* (kills property-mutants). GPT-4 "was able to automatically synthesize correct PBTs for **21%** of properties extractable from API documentation," the best model needed **2.4 samples** to produce one valid-and-sound PBT, and the automatic soundness metric agreed with humans at 100% precision / 97% recall ([arXiv 2307.04346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04346), search-summary). Later work attacks vacuous properties directly ("Beyond Superficial Tests," ACL Findings 2026) and the Hypothesis maintainers' Agentic PBT system shifts the design to many cheap properties with a second Claude Opus 4.1 call scoring each bug report against a published rubric ([agentic-pbt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mmaaz-git/agentic-pbt/main/README.md), repo). The same anchoring problem appears in oracle research under the name "Oracles That Cannot Fail" ([arXiv 2608.17214](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17214), title only), and in metamorphic testing, where natural-language relations for NLP showed **true-positive 21% against false-positive 23%** ([Terragni ICSME 2025](https://valerio-terragni.github.io/assets/pdf/cho-icsme-2025.pdf), search-summary). The most robust oracle designs in the notes do not ask the LLM for a novel oracle at all: MR-Adopt lifts a human's hard-coded example pair into a relation applicable to all inputs for **72% of encoded MRs** ([ACM 10.1145/3691620.3696020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3691620.3696020), peer-reviewed, search-summary), and sanitizer/crash oracles need no assertion at all.

Up the ladder, the failure mode is vacuity and the metric is reach. QuartetFuzz's analysis of OSS-Fuzz-Gen benchmarks found that **57–65% of crashes initially flagged as bugs were spurious**, unreachable from documented calling contexts because the LLM-written harness violated API preconditions; its own adversarial-validation stage "intercepted 58 harness-induced crashes" and reached a **4.8% false-positive rate** on 42 reports ([arXiv 2605.21824](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.21824v1), search-summary). In bounded model checking, verify-rust-std merged LLM-generated Kani contracts in August–October 2025, and PR #623 (August 2026) found two harnesses from that batch constructing `IndexRange::new_unchecked(start, end)` with unconstrained inputs, violating the `start <= end` precondition, hidden because CI ran with `--no-assert-contracts` ([verify-rust-std PR #623](https://github.com/model-checking/verify-rust-std/pull/623), repo). In spec synthesis, **34.6% of specifications that pass verification are incomplete** relative to human ones, typically by merging branch-specific conditions into a `True` precondition or a one-sided bound ([arXiv 2608.13077](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.13077), search-summary); Microsoft's AutoCLRS project (Claude Opus 4.6 writing >100k lines of F*/Pulse for 52 algorithms) needed ~5,000 lines of spec audited by hand and found "vacuous complexity proofs," and a follow-up using small verified tests exposed spec gaps in **8 of 52 algorithms** ([RiSE AutoCLRS](https://risemsr.github.io/blog/2026-03-06-autoclrs/); [SPOTs](https://risemsr.github.io/blog/2026-04-16-spotting-specs/), repo blog). Lahiri's intent-formalization paper proposes the remedy as metrics: *soundness* (spec satisfied on every test pair) and *completeness* (fraction of output mutations the spec rejects), which exposed a GPT-4 Dafny spec missing a bi-implication and mislabeled specs human labelers had passed ([RiSE blog](https://risemsr.github.io/blog/2026-03-05-shuvendu-intent-formalization/), repo blog). CLEVER (NeurIPS 2025) goes further and requires the model to prove its generated spec equivalent to a hidden ground-truth spec, ruling out weakened specs by construction ([clever](https://github.com/trishullab/clever), repo).

The two most instructive operational designs for reach accounting are Antithesis's agent skills and the P language's spec-inference toolkit. Antithesis's workload skill makes the agent "assert the precondition, not the violation" and treats `Sometimes` assertions that never fire as "evidence the workload isn't reaching its targets," with an anti-vacuity rule that "no reach claim is the negation of an Always ... at the same site"; its mutation-testing skill applies one realistic mutant per property and classifies survivors as Bad Mutant, Bad Oracle ("a success, not a detour"), Workload Gap or Unfalsifiable Property, while excluding `Sometimes` assertions from mutation because they "fail only when [the] condition is false in every timeline" ([antithesis-workload](https://github.com/antithesishq/antithesis-skills/blob/main/antithesis-workload/SKILL.md); [antithesis-mutation-testing](https://github.com/antithesishq/antithesis-skills/blob/main/antithesis-mutation-testing/SKILL.md), repo). The P maintainer's PR #974 keeps an explicit ledger: **82 LLM-proposed invariants, 50 verified by PChecker, 0 confirmed bugs, 6 monitor-encoding bugs, 9 spurious**, "avoiding 15 false reports," with "auto-canary" vacuity probes that replace each assertion with `assert false` ([P PR #974](https://github.com/p-org/P/pull/974); [PR #977](https://github.com/p-org/P/pull/977), repo). Zero real bugs and fifteen avoided false reports on tutorial benchmarks is the field's clearest statement that the engineering problem is adjudication, not generation.

## LLM-written harnesses reach human parity, and the generator may matter more than the search

The harness-generation literature is the most mature part of the driver paradigm and it has converged on a four-stage feedback ladder: compile errors fed back for repair (every system since OSS-Fuzz-Gen 2023), first-run crashes fed back, coverage deltas used to re-select APIs or mutate prompts, and finally crash triage for harness-induced false positives, the stage where 2025–2026 work concentrates. Google's OSS-Fuzz-Gen produced valid new targets for **160 C/C++ projects** with a maximum **29% line-coverage gain** over human targets and **30 new bugs** including OpenSSL CVE-2024-9143, bugs that "could only have been discovered with newly generated targets" ([oss-fuzz-gen](https://github.com/google/oss-fuzz-gen), repo). Zhang et al. (ISSTA 2024) generated **736,430 drivers for 86 questions at ~$8,000 and fuzzed them for 3.75 CPU-years**, solving 91% of questions but noting LLMs "face difficulties ... for APIs with intricate specifics" ([ACM 10.1145/3650212.3680355](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3650212.3680355), peer-reviewed, search-summary). PromptFuzz (CCS 2024) reached **1.61× OSS-Fuzz branch coverage** and 33 confirmed bugs from 49 crashes with libFuzzer as the engine and coverage-guided prompt mutation ([PromptFuzz](https://github.com/PromptFuzz/PromptFuzz), repo). QuartetFuzz (2026) is the first to tie a per-harness cost to human parity: **+0.03 pp line / −0.07 pp branch versus human "Gold" harnesses**, at roughly **$4.80 per harness with Claude Opus 4.6 or $0.40 with Gemini Flash** ([QuartetFuzz](https://github.com/OwenSanzas/QuartetFuzz), repo; comparison figures search-summary). Adjacent systems extend the pattern to grammars and specs: KernelGPT writes Syzkaller specs validated by `syz-check`, yielding **24 new kernel bugs and 11 CVEs** with specs merged upstream ([KernelGPT](https://github.com/ise-uiuc/KernelGPT), repo); ChatAFL extracts protocol grammars for AFLNet and found 9 previously unknown vulnerabilities ([ChatAFL](https://github.com/ChatAFLndss/ChatAFL), repo; figures search-summary); Eywa has the LLM build protocol models from RFCs for model-based testing and found **33 bugs, 16 new**, in DNS, BGP and SMTP implementations "despite extensive prior testing with manually crafted models" ([arXiv 2312.06875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06875), search-summary).

The most consequential single result for a coverage-guided PBT project is Gentoo (Vikram and Padhye, 2026): an agent with terminal access iteratively synthesises JQF/Zest input generators for seven Java libraries and achieves **11–21% higher branch coverage** than human generators on average, and "coverage guidance and mutation strategies are not statistically significantly beneficial for agent-synthesized generators, but is significant for all human-written generators" ([arXiv 2604.01442](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01442), search-summary). Read carefully, this says the LLM's value lies in encoding input structure, and that once the generator encodes semantics well enough, grey-box search adds little, at least on seven well-structured Java targets. It does not say coverage feedback is useless; it says the marginal value of coverage feedback depends on generator quality, which is an empirical question stream_fuzz is well placed to answer for the BEAM. A small Claude-4-sonnet study points the same way from the other side: PBT and example tests each caught **68.75%** of edge-case bugs and **81.25%** together, with PBT better on performance and broad-input bugs and examples better on specific boundaries ([arXiv 2510.25297](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25297), search-summary; n = 16).

For concurrency and deterministic simulation the peer-reviewed evidence is thin and the grassroots evidence is thick. No paper has an LLM write Coyote tests or Shuttle/Loom harnesses that a controlled scheduler then uses to find bugs; SyncPetri proposes Petri-net-guided LLM generation of Loom-compatible harnesses with bug counts unretrievable ([arXiv 2607.21530](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21530), search-summary). In repositories, 551 commits since 2025 carry both "Co-Authored-By: Claude" and `loom::model`; in moq, Loom "found no bug" but "mutation testing confirmed the models still catch their intended bugs," after a nightly job that timed out at 60 minutes until the models were bounded ([moq PR #4071](https://github.com/moq-dev/moq/pull/4071), repo); in Hawsey, Claude-written Coyote tests reproduced two real state-transition races and a third test "failed before the fix ... and passes after" ([Hawsey commit](https://github.com/Chris-Wolfgang/Hawsey/commit/d8513ae9404f23b4c0436599454f2efc7a74d7c6), repo). The Hypothesis project itself merged a Claude Code command in August 2026 because "the model would not propose a RuleBasedStateMachine even for classes whose methods obviously mutate them, and order-dependent bugs went unreached," and the command instructs pinning the minimal falsifying input as `@example(...)` ([hypothesis commit](https://github.com/HypothesisWorks/hypothesis/commit/3fc38f1d6a6b1752bf5fad61d44ee79d31d35406), repo). On the BEAM, Claude-co-authored commits added a `mix concuerror` task and interleaving tests exploring **198 interleavings** in roux, and verified a single-flight primitive "across all 4 interleavings (0 errors)" in fenrir ([roux](https://github.com/QuinnWilton/roux/commit/b3b61c795ae64c8b421464d5674716c1f4cffe7d); [fenrir](https://github.com/roquess/fenrir/commit/06b92b9784346fc5be9c5111ec227c93bcaf13a0), repo), illustrating that small BEAM state spaces make Concuerror a cheap exhaustive oracle for agent-written code. In each documented real-bug case the LLM wrote both the fix and the test, so the explorer acted as a reproduction and regression oracle rather than as a discovery engine (inference).

Design-level specs show the widest gap between benchmark and practice. Across 30 LLMs and 205 specs, natural-language-to-TLA+ generation reached **26.6% syntactic and 8.6% semantic correctness** ([arXiv 2606.05792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05792), search-summary), and Hillel Wayne's assessment is that LLMs are "excellent at fixing syntax errors in specs and summarizing error traces ... but very poor at generating meaningful properties from scratch" ([hillelwayne.com](https://www.hillelwayne.com/tags/formal-methods/), search-summary). Yet in September 2026 a Claude Opus 5.5-co-authored PR shipped five TLA+ specs, **56 recorded Go traces validated against them, 10 targeted mutations and seven findings** each with a counterexample and fix, running in CI in ~7 seconds each ([invowk PR #144](https://github.com/invowk/invowk/pull/144), repo). The reconciliation is that trace validation gives the agent a mechanical oracle (does the implementation's trace refine the spec?) and mutation gives it a second (does the harness notice when the implementation diverges?), which is the "expert oversight" the benchmark paper calls for, made executable. The same gradient runs through proof: off-the-shelf models verify **82% Dafny / 44% Verus / 27% Lean** on the vericoding benchmark ([arXiv 2509.22908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22908), search-summary) but only **40.3% / 24.7% / 7.8%** on AlgoVeri's aligned, non-trivial contracts ([algoveri](https://github.com/haoyuzhao123/algoveri), repo; figures search-summary), and LHC shows a fine-tuned **3B** model with LiquidHaskell as oracle annotating up to **94%** of functions in hours ([ICSE 2025](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2025/icse-2025-research-track/115/Neurosymbolic-Modular-Refinement-Type-Inference), peer-reviewed, search-summary). The recurring lesson across rungs is that the engine's fast, cheap verdict lets a *smaller* model do the drafting, which is the strongest economic argument against the token-hungry-reader design.

## The market sells inspectors; Antithesis is the lone engine-backed driver with a mutation gate

TesterArmy is real, and it is an inspector. Founded in 2025 by Szymon Rybczak and Oskar Kwaśniewski, based in San Francisco and Warsaw and backed by Y Combinator, it closed a **~$1.2M pre-seed** with angels including Guillermo Rauch, Walden Yan and Charlie Cheever; teams "provide a URL or an installable mobile app, describe the desired user journey in plain language, and run a test within minutes," including flows with email or SMS verification codes, and it claims more than 50 clients including Resend and bolt.new ([Vestbee](https://www.vestbee.com/insights/articles/tester-army-raises-1-2-m), search-summary, vendor traction). It also advertises an open-source AI testing framework ([Software Testing Magazine](https://www.softwaretestingmagazine.com/news/testerarmy-raised-1-2m-to-build-an-open-source-ai-testing-framework/), search-summary), whose repository the notes could not open. There is no classical engine, no property, mutation or simulation oracle, and no published bug counts or false-positive rates; it sits in a crowded cohort with Momentic (**$15M Series A**, 24 Nov 2025, claiming "200 million steps and ... over 390,000 bugs" in a month, vendor), QA Wolf ($36M B), Spur, TestSprite, Octomind (reportedly discontinued May 2026, unverified) and Checksum, differentiated on self-healing and mobile support rather than oracle strength ([Reuters via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com,2025-11-24:newsml_NFC2kBmT:0-momentic-raises-15-million-in-series-a-to-eliminate-the-qa-bottleneck-slowing-software-delivery/); [Momentic](https://momentic.ai/blog/series-a), search-summary). The unit-test side of the market has the same shape: Qodo raised a **$70M Series B** in March 2026, while its open-source Qodo-Cover, the canonical "generate, execute, keep if passes and raises coverage" loop, was **archived on 15 June 2025** with no mutation testing ([Calcalist](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/r1qdnboswx), search-summary; [qodo-cover](https://github.com/qodo-ai/qodo-cover), repo). That loop is oracle-free: a wrong test that passes on buggy code is kept, which is why the cohort reports "bugs caught" counts rather than precision.

The engine-backed vendors are few. Antithesis raised a **$105M Series A led by Jane Street** in December 2025 on ~$10M ARR ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/jane-street-leads-antithesiss-105m-series-a-to-make-deterministic-simulation-testing-the-new-standard-302631076.html), search-summary) and in 2026 published an Apache-2.0 skills pack for Claude Code and Codex in which "agents own the workload and assertions; the engine owns search and fault injection," sequenced research → setup → workload → launch → mutation-testing ([antithesis-skills](https://github.com/antithesishq/antithesis-skills), repo). Its product page states the thesis directly: "AI coding agents are good at creating broad harnesses, but they can also create plausible tests that miss the real invariant" ([antithesis.com/product](https://antithesis.com/product/), vendor, search-summary). No numbers on bugs found by agent-written workloads were published. Code Intelligence's CI Spark (since September 2023) has an LLM identify entry points and write fuzz harnesses for C/C++, Java and JS/TS, and claims a wolfSSL vulnerability found with "no manual intervention — beyond setting up the project and typing `cifuzz spark`" ([CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/652029/code-intelligence-unveils-new-llm-powered-software-security-testing-solution.html); [Code Intelligence](https://www.code-intelligence.com/blog/ai-generated-fuzz-test-wolfssl-vulnerability), search-summary, vendor). Diffblue (Java only) added bring-your-own-LLM to its deterministic RL search in March 2026 ([Diffblue](https://www.diffblue.com/resources/announcing-the-next-generation-of-our-best-in-class-unit-test-generation-platform/), vendor, search-summary); Imandra's CodeLogician routes a LangGraph agent into the ImandraX prover and claims to close "a 41–47 percentage point accuracy gap compared to LLM-only reasoning" ([Imandra](https://www.imandra.ai/articles/imandra-releases-codelogician), vendor, search-summary). Mayhem was absorbed by Bugcrowd on 4 November 2025 ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/11/04/bugcrowd-acquires-ai-security-startup-mayhem-fuse-hacker-ingenuity-machine-intelligence/), search-summary). Tellingly, the AIxCC winners did not spin out harness businesses: Theori commercialised as Xint Code, marketed as "the first completely LLM-native SAST" ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/03/17/theori-launches-xint-code-ai-platform-uncover-hidden-vulnerabilities-massive-codebases/), search-summary), Trail of Bits open-sourced Buttercup, and Team Atlanta stayed MIT-licensed (inference on positioning).

Security is where the hybrid architecture is most explicit and best funded. XBOW (**$120M Series C at $1B+**, plus $35M in May 2026) states that "the AI agents that surface potential vulnerabilities are never the same systems that confirm them," using non-AI validators; an independent tally of its HackerOne reports found 132 resolved, 303 triaged and **~25% closed as informative or not applicable** ([Rawsec](https://blog.raw.pm/en/about-the-hype-around-xbow/), search-summary; [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/autonomous-offensive-security-firm-xbow-raises-120m-at-1b-valuation/), search-summary). Incumbents keep classical SAST for detection and use LLMs downstream: Semgrep Assistant reports 95–96% user agreement and 20–40% fewer findings to triage ([Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2025/announcing-ai-noise-filtering-and-triage-memories/), vendor, search-summary), and Semgrep's own test of Claude Code on real Python web apps found 46 vulnerabilities at a **14% true-positive rate**, pre-Opus 4.6 ([Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2025/finding-vulnerabilities-in-modern-web-apps-using-claude-code-and-openai-codex/), vendor, search-summary). The capital pattern is consistent: investors reward an oracle that is not an LLM (Antithesis, XBOW validators, Harmonic's Lean checker at a $1.45B valuation) or distribution at the pull request (CodeRabbit $60M, Greptile $25M, Graphite acquired by Cursor), while Tracxn shows AI-testing equity funding falling from **$39.2M across 5 rounds (through April 2025) to $5.54M across 2 rounds (through May 2026)** on a taxonomy that evidently excludes the Antithesis and XBOW rounds ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/trending-business-models/startups-in-ai-powered-software-testing/__8ghi89CHIh8xUvUJI5R79cnj_bakLuf8_Bg2F2l8tLc), search-summary).

The gaps are therefore specific. No company sells LLM-authored property-based tests with shrinking gated by a mutation score; no vendor publishes a mutation score for its generated tests, and the only LLM-mutant tool is the ~300-star open-source Mutahunter ([mutahunter](https://github.com/codeintegrity-ai/mutahunter), repo). LLM-driven BMC and model checking exist only in papers (KaPilot, BMC-Agent, Vero) and in the P maintainer's own PRs. In-process concurrency exploration (Loom/Shuttle/Coyote/Concuerror-style) with an LLM writing the model has no vendor at all, Antithesis operating at whole-system level. By language, C/C++ and the JVM are best served, JS/TS is served for fuzzing by Code Intelligence and for E2E by the whole QA-agent cohort, Rust is academic only, Go has record/replay only, and **Elixir/BEAM has nothing**, despite mature PBT engines (StreamData, PropEr, PropCheck) and an exhaustive concurrency checker (Concuerror). The notes' inference, which this report endorses, is that the buyer expectation the security side has set, that a finding comes with a crash or proof-of-concept, transfers naturally to correctness testing via counterexample-producing engines, and that a shrunk falsifying input is to a property what a PoV is to a vulnerability.

Two of the "popularisation artifacts that don't exist" remain missing. There is still no bugs-per-CPU-hour benchmark running example, property, coverage-guided, concolic and BMC on the same dated bug corpus; the notes repeatedly record that no study prices fuzzing CPU-hours against LLM tokens, CyberGym merely requires cost reporting on its leaderboard, and PBT-Bench (2026) benchmarks agents writing PBTs rather than rungs against each other ([pbt-bibliography](https://github.com/ngernest/pbt-bibliography), repo). And there is no one-page ladder taxonomy. Both are cheap relative to their leverage.

For stream_fuzz specifically, the design document (dated 16 Sep 2026) positions the project as "the first rung past vanilla PBT," keeps StreamData as a dependency, uses OTP `:cover` line deltas plus `StreamFuzz.event/1` and `StreamFuzz.target/1` as novelty signals, persists a distilled corpus, and lists stateful campaigns, schedule exploration and concolic backends as non-goals; the README's own preamble concedes the find-rate claim is unproven. Six implications follow from the evidence, in rough order of cost.

First, the README caveat is the field's gap, and the project already holds the seed of the answer: `eval/decode_tag_properties_test.exs` is a planted-bug suite, and the design doc's success metric ("more distinct failing properties per CPU-hour than `max_runs: 10_000` uniform baseline on a fixed corpus of seeded bugs") is exactly the bugs-per-CPU-hour experiment nobody has published for any PBT rung. Gentoo's finding that coverage guidance stops being statistically significant once an agent-written generator encodes input structure ([arXiv 2604.01442](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01442), search-summary) is a direct threat to the premise and should be the first thing tested: run the planted-bug corpus under uniform StreamData, under stream_fuzz, and under stream_fuzz with LLM-rewritten generators, and publish all three numbers. Whichever way it comes out, it is a result the literature lacks for a non-Java, non-Python ecosystem.

Second, `StreamFuzz.event/1` is already a `Sometimes` assertion by another name. Antithesis's rule that unfired reach claims "are evidence the workload isn't reaching its targets" and P's vacuity canaries suggest the cheapest high-value feature: a per-campaign vacuity report listing events declared but never fired and properties whose coverage delta saturated within the first N examples. This converts the novelty signal the project already collects into the adequacy metric the ISSTA 2026 papers say is missing from coverage alone (inference).

Third, mutation-graded properties is the open niche the notes identify most sharply: Property-Based Mutation Testing (ICST 2023) formalises killability of a property, LLM-PBT generation exists since 2023, and "nobody appears to have published the combination" ([arXiv 2301.13615](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13615), search-summary; notes' inference). The Elixir mutation tool listed in awesome-mutation-testing is JordiPolo's "mutation" and the design doc names muex; following Antithesis's one-realistic-mutant-per-property protocol and classifying survivors as Bad Mutant / Bad Oracle / Workload Gap / Unfalsifiable would give stream_fuzz a quality gate no commercial PBT product has, and would directly address the "hundreds of completely pointless tests" critique ([HN 47234917](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47234917), search-summary). The replicability study's warning applies: mutation guarantees non-vacuity, not correctness of the oracle, so properties must be written against documentation or a model, never against the implementation in the prompt.

Fourth, the LLM's best role for this project is the one the ablations assign it: write and repair properties and generators, and triage engine output. The precedent is the Hypothesis project's own Claude Code command ([hypothesis commit](https://github.com/HypothesisWorks/hypothesis/commit/3fc38f1d6a6b1752bf5fad61d44ee79d31d35406), repo) and Antithesis's skills pack; a `stream_fuzz` skill that teaches an agent to mine properties from `@doc` and typespecs, to prefer sanitizer-free oracles (roundtrip, idempotence, model comparison) over asserted values, to pin shrunk counterexamples as regression examples, and to score failures against a rubric before a human sees them (as Agentic PBT does with a second Opus call) is cheap and consistent with every result above. Smaller models suffice for drafting when the engine's verdict is fast, per LHC and Team Atlanta.

Fifth, the stateful gap is real and BEAM-specific. StreamData has no state-machine testing; PropEr and PropCheck do; Eywa shows an LLM can write the protocol model for model-based testing and find 16 new bugs in heavily tested implementations ([arXiv 2312.06875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06875), search-summary); and Concuerror gives an exhaustive oracle for interleavings in the small state spaces typical of OTP code (4–198 interleavings in the grassroots examples). The design doc already asks that the corpus format "plug in later" for stateful; an LLM-written `statem` model checked by PropCheck and, for the concurrent parts, by Concuerror would be the first engine-backed LLM tooling for the BEAM, a white space the commercial survey confirms. The Hypothesis maintainers' observation that models do not propose state machines unprompted means the skill must say so explicitly.

Sixth, Elixir 1.20 (June 2026) ships gradual set-theoretic typing with "verified bugs ... guaranteed to fail at runtime if executed, with an extremely low false positives rate" and function signatures as the next milestone ([Elixir v1.20](https://elixir-lang.org/blog/2026/06/03/elixir-v1-20-0-released/), search-summary). That is a free, always-on rung above tests requiring no LLM artifact today, and once signatures land it opens an LHC-style "LLM writes signatures, compiler judges" loop that no one has reported for Elixir or Erlang.

Of the user's three "to actually build" (JS/TS unified harness with concolic backend, diff-scoped mutation with LLM equivalence filtering, example-to-property promotion), the evidence ranks the second highest: every component exists with measured numbers (ACH 0.95/0.96, fine-tuned UniXcoder equivalent-mutant detection at **F1 86.58%** on MutantBench ([arXiv 2408.01760](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.01760), peer-reviewed, search-summary), Stryker and Mutahunter diff scoping) and no vendor has assembled them. Example-to-property promotion ranks second: MR-Adopt's 72% shows the lifting step works for metamorphic relations and the ISSTA 2026 misguidance result argues for deriving properties from examples a human wrote rather than from the implementation. The JS/TS concolic harness ranks lowest in near-term promise: no LLM+CrossHair work exists in any language, the 8.6% TLA+ semantic-correctness figure is a warning about how models handle formal targets without a fast oracle, and XState v6 has just occupied the adjacent model-based-testing slot with human-authored code.

## Conclusion

The question "driver or inspector" turns out to be less about recall than about the form of the output. A token-hungry model reading code produces a claim; a model writing a harness, property, mutant, model or spec produces an artifact that an engine turns into a reproducer, and reproducers are what maintainers, bounty programs, AIxCC's scoring rule and investors have all independently decided to pay for. The frontier-model results of 2026 do not overturn this: they show models have become excellent proposers, and every pipeline that reports them still buys an executed verdict before filing. What the literature has not done is price the two paradigms against each other on the same targets, and the one clean attribution available (48 fuzzer PoVs to 25 LLM-module PoVs inside Atlantis) suggests the right framing is complementary coverage, with the model finding format-aware bugs the fuzzer cannot and the fuzzer finding the long tail the model cannot afford to read.

The second shift is that the verification burden has moved one level up. Mutation score exposed vacuous tests; now misguidance exposes mutation score, harness-induced crashes expose fuzzer verdicts, under-constrained inputs expose BMC passes, and incomplete-but-verifying specs expose proofs. At every rung the fix is the same: a second engine check that the first engine's instrument is itself reaching the interesting region, whether a fired `Sometimes`, a killed per-property mutant, an enabled contract assertion or a hidden-spec equivalence proof. For stream_fuzz this means the project's differentiator is not the scheduler but the ledger it can keep: which properties reached what, which survived which mutants, and how many bugs per CPU-hour each rung bought. A BEAM project that publishes that ledger, with an LLM drafting the properties and models and the engine signing the verdicts, would fill the single ecosystem gap the commercial survey found completely empty and would supply the benchmark the user's September notes asked for and the field still lacks.
